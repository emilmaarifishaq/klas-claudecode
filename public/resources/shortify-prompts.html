01 · Mine the moments

Most of the video is not the video.

A fifteen-minute recording has maybe four moments that earn a short. The rest is throat-clearing, setup, and tangent.

So before anything renders, the system reads the timestamped transcript and finds them: the claim with a number in it, the belief it overturns, the before-and-after.

It skips the intro on purpose, because the first thing you said is almost never the strongest thing you said.

The input is a timestamped transcript plus the screen recording.
The intro gets skipped. The hook lives in the middle.
One pass finds the moments. Everything downstream builds on them.

03 · The hook

The whole video lives or dies in one second.

Short-form is won or lost in the first second, so the hook is the one thing you never improvise.

Every hook is instantiated from a vault of patterns that have already proven they stop a scroll. You keep the proven shape and swap only the variables: the tool, the number, the outcome.

On screen it shows as a title of three or four words, never a sentence that spans the frame. Spoken, it promises a payoff without giving it away.

04 · The script

Say the payoff first.

A short is not a slow reveal. The result goes at the front, and the mechanism explains it after.

The script always answers why, not just what. Not "I built a dashboard," but "now I never lose a lead again." The pain it removes is the point.

And it names things. The actual model, the actual feature, never a vague "a new AI tool." Specificity is what makes it sound true.

There is no spoken call to action. The CTA lives on the end card, so the script stays pure value the whole way through.

Result before mechanism. Never bury the payoff.
Answer why: the pain it removes, not just the feature.
Name the model and the feature. Never "AI."
No spoken CTA. The end card carries it.
Prompt 04 · write the three scripts
Write the spoken script for each angle in angles.md. Keep each under 30 seconds when read aloud.

Rules that apply to all three:
- Open with the hook already written for that angle.
- Result before mechanism. Lead with the outcome, explain how only after.
- Answer WHY: name the pain it removes ("now I do not have to..."), not just what the thing does.
- Name the model and the feature specifically (e.g. Opus 4.8). Never say "AI" or "AIs." Say "Claude Code," "Claude," or "agents."
- Write in thought-lines with natural pauses, the way a person actually talks.
- No spoken call to action. The end card handles that.

Keep the three shapes distinct: proof leads with the win, contrarian leads with the wrong belief, transformation leads with the old-world cost. Save each to its own script file and show me before anything renders.

06 · B-roll and screencap

Your product is the star.

This is the make-or-break step. Most AI shorts fail here, drowning the screen in generated art that means nothing.

The rule is the opposite. The real thing you built, the actual screen recording, carries most of the beats, shown early and cut through panel by panel as the script names them.

Generated art is the spice, not the meal: a conceptual opener, and a few canonical scenes built once and reused across all three cuts.

A list line ("watch every line, approve every step, catch every mistake") becomes rapid word cards, one per item, never a full sentence on screen.

Prompt 06 · the beat-by-beat plan
Build the visual plan for each short, beat by beat against the script.

Generated art is the spice, not the meal:
- One conceptual opener.
- A small set of canonical scenes (build them once, reuse the exact same ones across all three angles).

Other sources:
- A list line gets one rapid word card per item, four words max. A full sentence is never a word card.
- The cold open is a short, relevance-matched library clip, before the product appears.
- End on a wide shot of the full product.

Motion variety: no camera move more than twice in a row. Then verify the actual frames, do not trust the plan blindly. A plain editor or notes screen is never the visual when the line is not about that.
